## Abstract

Protein language models (pLMs) are transformers over amino-acid sequences. Models trained
on sequence alone encode structure and function, from residue contacts and secondary
structure to binding sites and stability, and SAE-based interpretability metrics are used to
measure how well a model's features capture it. Such metrics are typically validated by
showing that the score is large and survives multiple-comparison correction. We show that
this is insufficient: a metric of this kind can rise when the relation it names is removed
from the training data.

We define a co-activation statistic, Lstruct, adapted from published pLM interpretability
metrics, and evaluate it on a controlled pair of masked- and causal-objective models sharing
initialisation, corpus and batch order. The masked arm scores higher at every depth, and the
result survives seven checks spanning dictionary training, reconstruction quality,
composition, architecture and the metric's own definition. The inference is nonetheless
wrong. Retrained on a corpus with residue order destroyed, Lstruct rises in every
model-by-depth combination tested; an untrained network matches it; direct probes favour the
opposite arm in 27 of 27 comparisons; and a single amino-acid indicator, using no model and
no SAE, outscores every learned feature in five of six conditions. A published structural
statistic, reimplemented from its Methods, fails the same control. What survives is a
training-invariant floor that neither corpus nor budget moves.

## 1 Introduction

Protein language models (pLMs) are transformers over amino-acid sequences, with residues as
tokens. Models trained on sequence alone encode structure and function, from residue
contacts and secondary structure to binding sites and stability (Rives et al., 2021; Rao et
al., 2021). Zhang et al. (2024) argue that pLMs capture coevolutionary statistics of
interacting sequence motifs, not protein physics. Sparse autoencoders (SAEs) are one
instrument for asking what a model encodes. The SAE decomposes a model's activations into a
large dictionary of sparse features, and each feature is then scored against protein
annotations or structures (Simon & Zou, 2025; Adams et al., 2025; Gujral et al., 2025).

Two kinds of score are in use, and they differ in what can validate them.
Annotation-referenced scores compare a feature's activations against an externally defined
label, such as a Swiss-Prot site or a secondary-structure class, using F1 or AUROC (Simon &
Zou, 2025; Adams et al., 2025). They have a reference value: a perfect score is defined, and
the label exists independently of the feature. Relation-scored statistics ask instead whether
the residues a feature fires on stand in a specified geometric relationship to each other,
such as spatial proximity within a distance cutoff, with the null generated by permuting the
feature's own activations within each protein (Simon & Zou, 2025). No per-feature ground
truth exists and no value is defined as correct, so the score is accepted on indirect
grounds: it is large, it survives multiple-comparison correction, and it orders models in the
expected direction.

InterPLM publishes one metric of each kind. We report a case in which a relation-scored
statistic met every one of those grounds, along with seven further checks constructed against
it, and the inference drawn from it was still wrong.

**The control.** Build a second training corpus in which the relation the metric targets has
been destroyed while the nuisance quantities it might respond to instead are preserved
exactly. Pretrain the same architecture on it under the same budget, fit the same dictionary,
and recompute. The prediction is one-sided and deliberately weak: the metric should not
systematically increase when the relation it depends on is absent from training. Our approach
removes sequence order and the sequence-to-structure correspondence while leaving composition
and length untouched, so part of what the metric legitimately responds to survives.

**Relation to prior work.** The manipulation itself is established. Sinha et al. (2021)
pretrained masked language models on word-order-permuted text and found that the resulting
models scored surprisingly well on some parametric syntactic probes, which they read as a
deficiency in how representations are tested for syntactic information. We apply the
manipulation to an interpretability metric rather than to downstream capability. The nearest
analogue in the probing literature is the control task of Hewitt & Liang (2019), which
randomises the labels to diagnose the probe's capacity; the control proposed here leaves the
labels alone and destroys the relation in the training distribution. The nearest existing
check in the SAE literature is the randomly initialised model: Simon & Zou (2025) run a
weight-randomised baseline, Heap et al. (2025) showed that aggregate interpretability scores
often fail to separate trained from random transformers, and Korznikov et al. (2026) develop
related checks. We run that check alongside ours, and §4.2 establishes that the two diagnose
different failures.

**The specimen.** Throughout, we use a comparison between two training objectives, masked and
causal, on a controlled pair of pLMs: one backbone instantiated twice, sharing initialisation
seed, corpus and batch order, with the attention mask and the loss built from it as the only
difference. Existing cross-model comparisons of SAE features in pLMs vary objective,
architecture, data and scale together, so feature-level differences cannot be attributed to
any one factor; the closest matched comparison equalises FLOPs or validation loss across
separately trained models (Cheng et al., 2024, Appendix D) and evaluates downstream
performance, whereas we match the training trajectory and use the pair to test a metric. Here
the objective is the sole candidate explanation, which makes the metric's failure attributable
rather than ambiguous. Section 5 states what we do and do not conclude from the comparison
itself.

**Contributions.** We introduce a training-distribution control for metrics that score a
relation. We build a metric of our own and subject it to seven checks spanning dictionary
training, reconstruction quality, composition, architecture and the metric's own definition,
all of which it survives. We then run our control alongside two existing baselines, and our
metric fails all three. Applied to five published metrics, the three attacks disagree with
each other, and running only one would have cleared several of the five. We show that a single
definitional choice, the sequence-separation cutoff, determines the sign of the
masked-versus-causal comparison, and that no value of it we tested separates real from
permuted training data.

## 2 Setup

### 2.1 The pair

We pretrained a 42.0M-parameter transformer in two arms: 30 pre-LN blocks, d_model = 320, 5
heads, SwiGLU, RoPE, and tied input and output embeddings, with the block shape anchored to
ESM-C (EvolutionaryScale). One backbone was instantiated twice, and the only difference
between arms was the attention mask and the loss built from it; initialisation seed, corpus
and batch order were shared. The masked arm used 15% masking with the standard 80/10/10
split; the causal arm predicted the next residue. Both arms were pretrained on three million
UniRef50 sequences **[release version TBC]** at a maximum sequence length of 512 and a batch
size of 32. Three seeds per arm gave six models, trained for 40,283 steps and approximately
660M tokens, or 15.7 tokens per parameter; a scaling arm ran to 500 tokens per parameter, a
32-fold increase in budget, at **[n]** seed(s) per arm.

The pair does not hold the number of supervised positions fixed: the masked arm receives a
target at 15% of positions and the causal arm at every position. Equalising supervised
positions instead of tokens leaves the effect in place (§3).

Dictionary fitting and evaluation used a separate set of 1,500 SCOPe 2.08 domains filtered at
40% sequence identity (Chandonia et al., 2022), for which experimental structures are
available. The feature matrix holds one row per residue, using the first 510 residues of each
domain, for 293,760 rows; residue labels were measured on the untruncated 295,240 residues.
Nine depths were sampled, indexed by block and by position among the sampled depths; both
indices are used throughout:

| Block | 0 | 4 | 7 | 11 | 14 | 18 | 22 | 26 | 29 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Depth | 0% | 13% | 25% | 38% | 50% | 63% | 75% | 88% | 100% |

A cell is one (arm, depth, seed) combination. The corpus control (§2.3) ran at blocks 11, 14
and 18, at three seeds per arm.

### 2.2 The dictionary and the statistic

Lstruct adapts the structural-clustering analysis of Simon & Zou (2025), which compares a
feature's mean activation over spatial neighbours against a within-protein permutation null.
We depart from their settings in two ways: a contact radius of 8 Å rather than 6 Å, following
the CASP contact definition (Monastyrskyy et al., 2018), and a minimum sequence separation of
|i − j| ≥ 12 rather than none, which excludes local backbone contacts including those internal
to α-helices (Marks et al., 2011; Morcos et al., 2011) and retains the medium- and long-range
classes on which contact prediction is conventionally assessed. Contacts are measured between
Cα rather than Cβ atoms.

The active set A_f is the set of residues above the 90th percentile of feature f's activation
across the evaluation set, and the structural neighbourhood is

> N(i) = { j : ‖Cα_i − Cα_j‖ < 8 Å and |i − j| ≥ 12 }  (1)

so, with ā_f(i) the mean activation of f over N(i),

> Lstruct(f) = ( E_{i∈A_f}[ā_f(i)] − E_null[ E_{i∈A_f}[ā_f(i)] ] ) / SD(a_f)  (2)

the null taken over five permutations of f's activations within each protein, and SD(a_f) the
standard deviation of f's raw activations across the evaluation set. Section 3 varies the
separation cutoff; §4.5 evaluates Simon and Zou's statistic at their own settings. The
statistic is not a Cohen's d: the numerator is in neighbour-averaged units and the denominator
is the standard deviation of raw activations.

Five permutations are few enough that the null estimate affects absolute levels. Raising it to
25 permutations across 27 cells shifts mean Lstruct by +0.0073, never by more than 0.0187, and
changes no cell's sign. Absolute levels below should therefore be read as approximate; every
between-condition comparison is unaffected.

Two symbols are used for between-arm comparisons, on different scales. Δ is the raw
masked-minus-causal difference in mean Lstruct over all 2,560 features; the fold-disjoint refit
of §2.4 gives Δ = +0.0141. d is the standardised between-arm effect size over the three seed
means, giving d = +0.2568 for the same comparison. The two differ by roughly a factor of
eighteen, and each figure below is labelled with the symbol it belongs to.

### 2.3 The shuffled corpus

We permuted residues within each sequence of the pretraining corpus at construction,
independently per sequence across the same three million sequences, from a single random
stream. Length and per-sequence amino-acid composition are preserved exactly, since a
permutation reorders the same multiset, while all order is destroyed, and with it the
correspondence between sequence position and spatial position. Both arms trained on the same
shuffled corpus under the same budget; dictionary fitting, evaluation set and the Lstruct
implementation were identical to the native runs, and structural evaluation still uses real
experimental structures.

Relative to their natively trained counterparts, causal held-out perplexity rises 10.6% and
masked pseudo-perplexity 11.1%. The near-equal degradation rules out one arm having learned
the shuffled corpus substantially better than the other.

We tested one destruction procedure, and a second permutation seed would not constitute a
different one: composition and length are fixed by construction, so any seed gives a
statistically near-identical corpus (§5).

### 2.4 A leak in the evaluation split

The dictionary was fitted on 1,350 domains and Lstruct was scored on all 1,500, so every
fitting domain also appeared in the scored set. The original 150-domain holdout did not fix
this: 118 of them (78.7%) share a SCOPe fold with a fitting domain.

Scoring on those 150 domains alone reduces the effect by 30% but leaves it significant
(d = +0.1797, 95% CI [0.1105, 0.2489]). A fold-disjoint replacement, in which none of the 151
held-out domains shares a fold with a fitting domain, reproduces the arm separation to within
0.4% across all 18 refitted cells (Δ = +0.01410 against +0.01415). The leak inflates the effect
size but does not produce it. Figures below are computed on the original split unless stated;
Appendix M gives the fold-disjoint values and the construction.

## 3 Confounds that do not account for the effect

Lstruct is higher in masked than in causal models: d = +0.2568, seed means 0.2413 / 0.2455 /
0.2837, 95% CI over the three seed means [0.1987, 0.3149]. A two-sided t-test on the three seed
means (SD 0.02336, SE 0.01349, t = 19.04, df = 2) gives p = 0.0027, and seven of nine depths
survive Benjamini–Hochberg correction. Under a prediction-matched protocol that equalises the
number of supervised positions instead of tokens, all 18 seed-by-depth cells are significant.

We then constructed seven checks, each testing whether the difference could arise without
either model having learned more structure. Depths are given by block index throughout.

**Dictionary seed.** Is the effect dictionary-fitting noise? Across six arm-by-depth cells at
three dictionary seeds, the median seed-to-seed SD of mean Lstruct is 0.00034, against 0.00093
across three model seeds computed the same way on the same cells — 37% of it, and far below
Δ = 0.0141. Three seeds make the SD estimate itself noisy.

**A frozen shared dictionary.** Do the two arms learn different dictionaries? Forcing both arms
onto one dictionary under a frozen decoder, a frozen encoder and a soft-frozen variant
(Korznikov et al., 2026) leaves Δ positive in all nine conditions, across three depths and three
variants. It grows at the two shallower depths and shrinks at the deepest: block 7
+0.0146 → +0.0217, block 14 +0.0167 → +0.0241, block 22 +0.0072 → +0.0063. A shared crosscoder
(Minder et al., 2025) gives the same direction and decays with depth, at +0.0209, +0.0160 and
+0.0009 for blocks 7, 14 and 22. This condition runs at blocks 7, 14 and 22 rather than the 11,
14 and 18 of the corpus control, sharing only block 14. The frozen conditions were run without a
bootstrap and carry no confidence intervals.

**No dictionary at all.** Does the SAE create the effect? Recomputing Lstruct with no
autoencoder anywhere, taking each of the 320 residual-stream dimensions as a feature in place of
the 2,560 SAE latents, leaves Δ at +0.0084, +0.0056 and +0.0022 at blocks 11, 14 and 18 —
positive at every depth, and in the same depth ordering as the SAE version.

**Reconstruction quality.** Across 27 seed-by-depth cells the effect correlates with the
between-arm explained-variance gap at ρ = +0.475. The correlation is confined to the two
shallowest depths: over the remaining seven, ρ = −0.043 (p = 0.85). We report both, and do not
exclude the shallow cells. They are where the effect itself is near zero — the two arms differ by
0.00102 at block 0 against 0.0161 at block 11 — and they are also the only two depths that fail
Benjamini–Hochberg correction. The confound is concentrated where there is no effect to confound.

**Amino-acid composition.** Does the metric track which residues a feature fires on rather than
where they sit? For each feature we computed a background-corrected selectivity score, 0 for a
feature spread evenly across the 20 residue types and approaching 1 for one firing on a single
type (Appendix H). We predicted it would correlate positively with Lstruct. It does not: Spearman
ρ over all 2,560 features is −0.044 on the masked arm and −0.122 on the causal. The same scores,
on the same features and evaluation set, correlate with Lseq — Equation 2 with N(i) replaced by
{i−2, i−1, i+1, i+2} (Appendix D) — at −0.676 and −0.412. The two statistics differ only in the
neighbourhood, so a confound acting through the dictionary or the evaluation set could not
separate them. Section 4.4 establishes the complementary fact: the correlation across features is
weak, and the level a single composition feature can reach is high.

**Bidirectional access.** The remaining account is architectural rather than statistical. The
masked model attends to residues on both sides of a contact and the causal model does not, so the
masked arm might score higher because it can see the contact at all. That account predicts an
asymmetry: the masked advantage should be larger for contacts whose partner lies downstream of the
active residue, which the causal arm cannot attend to, than for upstream contacts, which it can. We
split each feature's structural neighbourhood by contact direction and took the between-arm
interaction, coded so that a positive value favours downstream contacts (Appendix K). It is
−0.1422, negative at all nine depth-by-seed cells, p = 0.004. The sign is the opposite of the
prediction, so the account is contradicted rather than merely unsupported.

**The separation cutoff.** Is the effect an artefact of setting the cutoff at 12? Holding model,
dictionary and evaluation set fixed and varying only the cutoff, the between-arm effect size at
block 11 moves from d = −2.2096 at gap 1 to d = +0.3425 at gap 12, crossing zero at gap 5 and
plateauing past gap 6; deeper blocks cross earlier, at gaps 4 and 3 for blocks 14 and 18. A cutoff
tuned to the result would appear as a peak, and gap 12 is not one — it sits within a wide flat
region, and the sweep maximum lies at gap 24 or beyond. The positive result does require excluding
|i − j| < 5, so the cutoff sets the sign of the comparison rather than merely its size. The sweep is
computed at a 6 Å contact radius rather than the 8 Å used elsewhere, so its absolute values are not
comparable with the rest of this section. Section 4.1 uses the same sweep for a different contrast,
between corpora rather than between arms. The full 48-row sweep is in Appendix F.

Subject to the qualifications above, the effect survives all seven checks. It is large, holds across
three model seeds, survives multiple-comparison correction at seven of nine depths, and orders the
two arms consistently. Section 4 is what happens when the result is asked one further question.

## 4 Evidence that the inference is invalid

Sections 4.1 to 4.4 are four independent lines against Lstruct. Section 4.5 shows that a published
statistic fails the same control, and §4.6 compares three attacks across six metrics.

### 4.1 It rises when order is removed from training

Lstruct is higher after order-destroyed training in 18 of 18 cells. Those are six model pairs at
three non-independent depths, so at the model level it is six of six, and an exact one-sided sign
test gives p = 0.016. Averaged over three seeds and blocks 11, 14 and 18, the causal arm moves from
a native 0.00240 to an order-destroyed 0.05201 (10.3–70.4× across cells) and the masked arm from
0.01655 to 0.02730 (1.31–2.11×).

The ratios are misleading without their denominators. The causal base is near zero, so the largest
ratios reflect a small denominator rather than a large shift. The direction does not depend on that:
all 18 differences are positive, and the smallest causal difference (+0.0437) exceeds the largest
masked one (+0.0156).

The scaling arm separates the effect from training budget. Over the 32-fold increase from 15.7 to
500 tokens per parameter (§2.1), the order-destroyed value moves only −4% to +23%, while native
values move by up to 116% and in opposite directions by arm. We call this the *training-invariant
floor*: the component of Lstruct that neither the training corpus nor the training budget can move.
A metric with such a floor will register that floor as structure whatever the model learned. One cell
in the scaling arm reverses, on the masked arm at block 14; it lies outside the 18-cell main-budget
grid and is the only measured cell in which the metric does not rise.

The rise persists across cutoffs, but its magnitude does not. The sweep of §3 also supports a second
contrast, between the native and order-destroyed corpora at fixed arm. The order-destroyed curve has
the same shape as the native one, shifted right: native crosses zero at gaps 5, 4 and 3 for blocks 11,
14 and 18, order-destroyed at gaps 16, 20 and 20, and by gap 30 the order-destroyed values exceed the
native ones at blocks 14 and 18. Across the swept range there is no cutoff at which native data shows
signal and permuted data shows none. A degree-preserving graph null gives the same conclusion from
another direction: at a swap rate of 0.80 the mean statistic runs −0.0238 to −0.0046, opposite in sign
to the real graph, so the positive signal is not reproduced by a graph matching each residue's contact
degree. Both are computed at the 6 Å radius of the sweep; Appendix F gives all 48 rows.

At gap ≥ 1, Simon and Zou's setting, the causal order-destroyed-to-native ratio falls from 10.41× to
1.85×, and on the masked arm below 1. The strict setting is where the failure appears; whether
strictness causes it is untested (Appendix F).

### 4.2 Training does not lift the metric above its random-initialisation level

The random-init check reveals a failure the corpus control misses. Simon & Zou (2025) run a
weight-randomised baseline and report that randomised models yield hundreds of features tied to
individual amino acid types while capturing no biological concepts. We run the same check on the
controlled pair.

An untrained network sits at about +0.018, flat across both architectures and all three blocks. The

Read together with §4.1, the direction of the account reverses: the masked arm's level does not require
training, while causal training moves the statistic an order of magnitude below the

for running both.

The same baseline separates the three structural measures reported here. Concept-F1 clears it cleanly at

trained range sits at the untrained floor while the top is well clear of it. Lstruct does not clear it at
all.

### 4.3 Direct probes favour the causal arm

With no SAE anywhere, linear probes for helix, strand and burial were fitted directly on the residue
representations. Values are masked minus causal, averaged over three seeds; helix, strand and burial are
test F1, contact is precision at L/5.

All three residue-label probes favour the causal arm at every depth and in every seed: 27 of 27 cells

Blocks 11 and 14 are two of the three depths at which the corpus control was run, and are where Lstruct
gives its largest masked advantage. The probes therefore disagree with the metric over two of its three
control depths.

The direct measure of structural content disagrees with the indirect one at the depths the claim rests on.
The disagreement is not a logical contradiction, since Lstruct measures spatial clustering of SAE features
while the probes measure linear decodability. Replacing the linear probe with an MLP, to test whether the
reversal is an artefact of linear separability, made all nine depth-by-label comparisons stronger and none
weaker, with the per-seed count still 27 of 27.

### 4.4 A single amino-acid indicator outscores almost every learned feature

Simon & Zou (2025) report that SAEs trained on weight-randomised pLMs still yield hundreds of features tied
to individual amino acid types, while capturing no biological concepts at all. We ask what such a feature
scores on a structural metric, using a no-model baseline: a synthetic layer of 29 indicator functions over
residue identity and its groupings, comprising 20 amino acids, 8 chemical classes and one all-ones column,
involving no model, no SAE and no training. Each indicator is substituted for a feature's activation vector
in Equation 2 and scored by the same code, with the same null and the same evaluation set.

Scored this way, aa:C, the indicator for cysteine, reaches +0.4221 against a best-of-2,560 of +0.087 to
+0.105 on the causal arm and +0.157 to +0.501 on the masked. It outscores every learned feature in five of
six cells, and in the sixth exactly one feature beats it.

Cysteine is the expected winner, and the runners-up say why. Disulfide bonds place cysteine pairs in spatial
contact at arbitrary sequence separation; the next seven indicators are the hydrophobic and aromatic residues
and their class groupings, which cluster in three dimensions through core packing. Both are chemical facts
about proteins that hold without any model. Because an indicator does not depend on the model, these values
are identical in every cell: on the causal arm, five of the 29 constants outscore all 2,560 learned features.

What this establishes is a level, not a mechanism: a quantity computable from residue identity alone can
reach most of the range the metric reports, without any model behind it. Section 3's composition check
establishes the complementary fact, that variation in composition across learned features does not track
Lstruct. Both hold, and together they say that the metric's scale is reachable by composition even though its
variation is not driven by it. A metric with that property cannot be read as a measure of learned structure on
magnitude alone.

### 4.5 A published structural statistic fails the same control

InterPLM (Simon & Zou, 2025) publish a structural-clustering statistic d_struct addressing the same
operational question as ours: they compare a feature's mean activation over residues within 6 Å against a null
of five within-protein permutations, testing with paired t-tests over 100 proteins per feature and retaining
features that survive Bonferroni correction at p < 0.05. The released repository does not implement it — the
only two files that mention it are dashboard modules — so we implemented it from the published Methods,
following those settings. One decision left open in that description changes the result; we report both
settings.

The procedure gates features at 0.6 of a maximum activation without specifying which maximum; the two readings
differ fifteen-fold in the analysed set, from a median of 1,485 of 1,500 proteins per feature to about 98, and
that difference propagates into every number the statistic produces (Appendix P).

| Arm | Mean d_struct, real | Shuffled | Significant features, real | Shuffled |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Causal | 0.4484 | 0.4672 | 985.7 | 1349.3 |
| Masked | 0.1376 | −0.0431 | 323.3 | 25.0 |

On the causal arm, models trained on permuted residues score higher on both the mean and the count of features
called significantly structural, 1,349 against 986. The masked arm runs in the opposite direction. Under the
dataset-wide gate the causal arm shows the same direction block by block (§4.6, and Appendix P, Table 2), at a
higher absolute level: +0.5326, +0.5800 and +0.5800 native against +0.6086, +0.7475 and +0.8713
order-destroyed. The two gates disagree on the level and agree on the failure, so the result does not turn on
the ambiguity. Absolute values are not comparable across gates and are not compared here.

d_struct is computed at 6 Å with no separation floor, which is the setting at which §3's cutoff check shows
Lstruct has no training-invariant floor. The two statistics therefore fail the control at different settings,
and the family resemblance is in the construction rather than in the failure.

InterPLM do not use d_struct to order models: in their Methods it is a per-feature triage statistic appearing
once in their results, on one model at one layer. There is no published number of theirs to invalidate, and the
bound falls on the reading rather than on their paper. Their second metric, concept-F1, passes our corpus
control on both arms, but from a low floor: across the 81 cells of the concept-F1 grid every value lies between
0.0090 and 0.0620, so the pass is weak evidence rather than none.

Three metrics carry a verdict on both the corpus control and the no-model layer. Lstruct fails both, and fails
the random-init check as well. Concept-F1 passes the corpus control and fails the no-model layer. d_struct splits
by arm, and its masked condition clears both, on a single seed and with a floor effect at one of its three blocks.
An unrun, undefined or underpowered cell is not read as a failure.

The attacks are therefore not ordered by severity, and two of them cannot substitute for each other even in
principle. The no-model layer scores 29 indicator functions that do not depend on any model, so its values are
identical whatever corpus a model was trained on; it can catch a metric whose scale is reachable without a model,
and can say nothing about what training does. The corpus control varies only the training distribution and holds
the scoring layer fixed, so it can catch a metric that rises when the relation is destroyed, and says nothing
about the level. Lstruct fails both, for different reasons.

The single-latent F1 row is an absence rather than a failure: the metric has no discriminative power on this
concept set for any of the five inputs, a finding about prevalence-dominated concept metrics rather than about the
models. Appendix J decomposes the 83–91% figure into its two modes.

## 5 Discussion

**What is identified.** The metric rises when the relation is removed from training, which removes the inference
that a higher Lstruct means more learned structure. Sections 4.2 and 4.3 narrow what it measures instead: the
quantity survives random initialisation and disagrees with direct decodability. The stronger claim, that it
measures nothing about structure, is not identified.

**Cost.** The control requires one additional pretraining run per arm at the original budget, and scales with the
model **[state approximate compute for one 42M run]**. It should be run once per metric on a small controlled pair
rather than once per model. The no-model layer requires no pretraining and caught concept-F1, which the corpus
control cleared; the corpus control caught d_struct, which the no-model layer cleared. The three are complements,
and the cheap ones do not substitute for the expensive one.

**Limitations.**

- One destruction procedure (§2.3). Replicating the treatment requires a structurally different destruction, such
  as a cross-sequence shuffle preserving only global composition, or a block shuffle preserving local windows,
  rather than another permutation seed.
- Training- and evaluation-distribution shift are not separated. Order-destroyed models are scored on natively
  ordered input, which is out of distribution for them. The control that separates the two costs one forward pass
  and has not been run.
- Coverage. The corpus control ran at three of thirty blocks, at blocks 11, 14 and 18.
- The untrained baseline is a single initialisation — for an untrained network the only quantity that varies.
- The denominator is untested. Lstruct divides by SD(a_f), and we have not recomputed it with a fixed or
  rank-based denominator, though the components are computed separately in the analysis code. Order-destroyed
  dictionaries have activation scales roughly an order of magnitude below their native counterparts; those scale
  ratios do not track the ratios in Lstruct and move in opposite directions between arms, which is evidence that
  the scale gap does not propagate through the denominator rather than a direct test of scale-invariance.
- Dictionary quality differs between conditions. Seven of the thirty order-destroyed cells carry a degeneracy flag
  set by the SAE pipeline, six of them on the masked arm and one on the causal. Order-destroyed dictionaries also
  reconstruct better than native ones (validation explained variance 0.99 against 0.89–0.98 masked, 0.88–0.91
  against 0.67–0.77 causal), so a dictionary-quality account of the rise remains open. The larger failure is on the
  causal arm, whose dictionaries carry one flag between them, and §4.4 shows the metric's level is reachable with no
  dictionary at all.
- The fold-disjoint split. Only the arm levels of §2.4 were recomputed on it. The corpus control of §4.1 is computed
  on the original split, where the contamination is common to both conditions being compared.
- d_struct coverage. The control is a single seed, and no untrained baseline was run for it.
- Perplexity is provisional. The masked-arm pseudo-perplexities depend on a mask-token identification corrected
  during analysis.
- One scale, one modality. A text model would require a different destruction procedure.

**Scope.** We claim that a metric can rise when the relation it names is destroyed, that seven checks spanning
dictionary training, reconstruction quality, composition, architecture and the metric's own definition did not
detect this, that a random-init baseline and a data control diagnose different things, and that one extra
pretraining run makes the failure visible. Nothing here claims that masked pretraining is better or worse than
causal at anything biological, nor that any metric here is worthless outside the conditions we ran.
